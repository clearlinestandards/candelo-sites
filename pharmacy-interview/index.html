The one line

“My value is my ability to step into complex, high-volume operations, quickly identify opportunities, develop the team, and implement sustainable workflows that improve performance and the member experience.”

Say it once, early. Everything after this is proof.

Opening — 90 seconds

“Tell me about yourself.”

Say this

I've spent the last three-plus years supervising pharmacy operations in the Diablo service area. I started with three pharmacies, took on a fourth, and the assignment was the same each time: a location that needed to perform better. I'd go in, find where the workflow was breaking, rebuild it with the team, and hold it there.

That's what got me sent to Oakland Main Hospital Pharmacy for two months — . And it's why I was asked to take Shadelands and Walnut Creek, two of the busiest pharmacies we run, filling about a day between them. Today I'm responsible for more than fifty people across those sites.

What I bring isn't just experience supervising. It's a repeatable way to walk into a complex, high-volume operation, find the opportunities fast, develop the team, and put workflows in place that hold after I move on.

Ends on the thesis. Do not run past two minutes.

Behavioral answers

Six questions, already answered

Each one is a skeleton — situation, action, result — keyed to a pillar. Fill the result with a real number and the answer is finished.

“Tell me about a time you turned around an underperforming location.”
Situation
Oakland Main Hospital Pharmacy. Two-month assignment, sent in because performance needed to improve.
Action
First week was observation, not changes — where the work stalled, where staff were doubling back, which step created the queue. Then one workflow change at a time, trained on the floor, with the leads owning it.
Result
— and it held after the assignment ended.
Pillar
Performance turnaround.
“Walk me through a day with competing priorities.”
Situation
Two call-outs, a system outage, a full waiter queue, and an escalated member — one morning, one of the busiest sites in the area.
Action
Triage in order: safety first, then the member in front of you, then throughput. Redeployed staff to the bottleneck, moved non-urgent work to later in the day, took the escalation yourself so the team stayed on the queue.
Result
.
Pillar
High-volume execution.
“Tell me about a difficult performance conversation.”
Situation
An employee whose attendance or output was affecting the rest of the team — and the team knew it.
Action
Private, specific, documented. Named the behavior and the impact, not the person. Set a clear expectation with a date, then followed up on that date instead of letting it drift.
Result
. Say what happened even if the answer was separation — owning that reads as stronger, not weaker.
Pillar
People development.
“Tell me about a change people resisted.”
Situation
A workflow that had been done the same way for years and was creating the backlog.
Action
Brought the staff who ran it into the fix instead of announcing it. Piloted it short, measured it, showed them their own numbers, adjusted the part they were right about.
Result
, and the team defended the new process to the next site.
Pillar
Change and process improvement.
“Tell me about an escalated member complaint.”
Situation
A member whose issue had already gone past the staff member in front of them.
Action
Took it off the counter, listened without defending, fixed what could be fixed that day, and told them exactly what would happen next — then closed the loop with the staff member so it wasn't just handled, it was taught.
Result
Resolved without further escalation; .
Pillar
Member experience.
“Tell me about someone you developed.”
Situation
A technician or lead with more capability than their role was using.
Action
Delegated something real with your name still on the outcome. Set the expectation, checked in on a schedule rather than hovering, gave feedback that was specific enough to act on.
Result
— and the site ran without you standing in it.

People development.

The closer

“Why should we hire you?”

Say this

Because you're not taking a risk on whether I can do this — I've already done it here, more than once, at increasing scale. Three pharmacies, then four, then Oakland Main, then the two busiest sites in the service area. Every time, the operation was better when I left it than when I got there: .

And I don't do it by being the person everything runs through. I build the team so the performance stays after I move to the next problem. That's what I'd bring to this role.

Ask them

Questions that make you sound like the peer, not the candidate

  • What does success look like in the first 90 days for whoever takes this?
  • Which metric is under the most pressure right now — and what's already been tried?
  • How much authority does this role have to change workflow directly, versus escalate a recommendation?
  • What's the widest gap between your strongest and weakest site, and what do you think is driving it?
  • Who would I be partnering with — pharmacists in charge, ops, HR — and where do those lines actually run day to day?

Traps

What loses the room

  • “I improved the metrics.” Unquantified claims read as no claim at all. Number, timeframe, mechanism — every time.
  • Describing the problem without the change. They don't need the diagnosis, they need what you did about it.
  • “We” for everything. Credit the team, then say the decision that was yours. Both, in that order.
  • The four-minute STAR answer. Cap it at two. Let them ask for more — that's the sign it landed.
  • Anything negative about a site or a leader. Say what the operation needed, never who failed it.
